Who emits, and how much per person?

More on population dynamics

The map above presents two data layers simultaneously: total emissions and emissions per person for each country. However, it draws our attention mostly to total emissions. While the largest total emittors are shifting from Europe and the USA to India and China, total emissions does not account for pollution intensity across regions of very different sizes. The underlying lifestyles, energy, and economic patterns are better represented by emissions per capita. The figures below more clearly emphasize how emissions per capita changes the carbon emissions story. Hover above any country to find it in every view. They all follow the year slider as well.

Population × emissions per person = total emissions

Biggest changes

From 1990 t0 2024, we see a reduction in emissions per capita for countries like the US, Russia, and Germany, while emissions per capita increase for countries like India and China. This causes the figure to change shape from a definitive slope to a more even spread. The causes of these changes include many complex factors, some of which are related to changing economies and technologies. For example, China's industry has grown significantly, while electrification around the world has picked up.

What this view leaves out

  • Where goods are made, not where they are used. Emissions are counted where they happen. A country that exports a lot carries emissions that serve other countries’ consumers.
  • Only 1990 to 2024. Countries that industrialised earlier have been emitting for far longer, so today’s totals say little about cumulative responsibility.
  • No land use and forestry. This data covers fossil CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide and F-gases. Land-use change is reported separately by EDGAR and is not shown here.
  • Averages hide inequality inside countries. Per-person figures are national means. Very small countries sit at the extremes, which is why rankings per person only include countries with at least 5 or 10 million people.
  • Simplified geography. The map uses coarse outlines, so the smallest countries and territories are missing from the map and from these views.
