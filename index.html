Who emits, and how much per person?

A different perspective

The map above presents two data layers simultaneously: total emissions and emissions per capita for each country. However, it draws our attention mostly to total emissions. While the largest total emittors are shifting from Europe and the USA to India and China, total emissions does not account for emission intensity across regions of very different sizes. The underlying lifestyles, energy, and economic patterns are better represented by emissions per capita. The figures below more clearly emphasize how emissions per capita changes the carbon emissions story. Hover above any country to find it in every view. They all follow the year slider as well.

Population × emissions per capita = total emissions

When represented like this, our attention might shift from focusing on absolute emittors to relative emittors. Countries like Saudi Arabia, Australia, the UAE, and the United States are relatively the largest emittors. This view introduces a conversation about underlying causes for emissions per country. Ask yourself, why does Saudi Arabia emit so much per person? Or what might be the difference between China and India? The answers include many complex factors related to changing (industrial) economies, population sizes, technologies, conflicts, and global trade patterns. For example, China and India are rapidly expanding industrial powers, while their populations are simultaneously growing. Saudi Arabia on the other hand is relatively small in its population but contributes roughly 10% of the world's oil.

What this view leaves out

  • Where goods are made, not where they are used. Emissions are counted where they happen. A country that exports a lot carries emissions that serve other countries’ consumers.
  • Only 1990 to 2024. Countries that industrialised earlier have been emitting for far longer, so today’s totals say little about cumulative responsibility.
  • No land use and forestry. This data covers fossil CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide and F-gases. Land-use change is reported separately by EDGAR and is not shown here.
  • Averages hide inequality inside countries. Per-person figures are national means. Very small countries sit at the extremes, which is why rankings per person only include countries with at least 5 or 10 million people.
  • Simplified geography. The map uses coarse outlines, so the smallest countries and territories are missing from the map and from these views.
